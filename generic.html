My Research

I develop large-scale, semi-numeric simulations of Cosmic Dawn in order to predict the cosmological 21-cm signal for both current and planned observational surveys.

Cosmic Dawn is the era of the universe's history in which the first stars and galaxies form, taking place within the first few hundred million years following the Big Bang. As these first sources turn on and emit radiation, they begin to ionize the neutral hydrogen gas pervading the young intergalactic medium in the first stages of cosmic Reionization. This era is key to our understanding of cosmology as the conditions of the early universe dictate the rate and intensity with which the first sources formed and began reionizing the cosmos. Further, observational data from Cosmic Dawn is currently scant due to vast cosmological distances, cosmic redshifting, and interlying foreground structures. I therefore develop self-consistent, large-scale simulations of Cosmic Dawn to study the impact that various assumptions and modelling techniques have on the observed 21-cm signal. Such theoretical predictions may then be used to guide future observational surveys and interpret their results, allowing us to place constraints on the astrophysical processes that dominated the early universe and better our understanding of cosmic evolution.

The first generation of stars to form in the early universe are known as Population III (PopIII) stars, characterized by their primordial composition. These first stars are believed to have formed within dark matter "minihalos" with virial masses of Mvir = 105 - 106 M☉. Due to their primordial composition, the avenues through which matter may cool and collapse to form PopIII stars are largely limited to atomic and molecular hydrogen transitions. Such transitions are relatively inefficent compared to those of heavier elements, meaning that PopIII stars are expected to reach much larger characteristic masses than modern metal-enriched stars, with some estimates even allowing for supermassive (~105 M☉) PopIII star formation. After PopIII stars form within a halo, they enrich it with their supernovae, leading to the formation of lower mass, enriched Population II (PopII) stars. Both PopII and PopIII stars illuminate the young universe with their radiation, heating and ionizing the intergalactic gas in the first stages of cosmic reionization. Much of Cosmic Dawn remains uncertain, however, due to the lack of observational data.
